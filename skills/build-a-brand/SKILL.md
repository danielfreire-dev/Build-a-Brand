# Build a Brand

Run a brand design engagement the way a senior designer would. Interview the user in rounds, recommending and pushing back, until you both share a clear picture of the brand. Then design it: propose distinct directions, refine one, show it in use on the user's own touchpoints (a landing page, a business card, a slide deck and so on), and write it up as a Brand Style Brief.

The goal is a brand that could only belong to this user. Most DIY brands end up as their category's average, or as a remix of someone famous, because nobody did the designer's work: finding what is true and specific about this brand and building from it. That work is your job.

**The arc:**
1. **Interview.** Rounds of questions until the strategy is clear. This is most of the session.
2. **Directions.** Three genuinely different concepts, each built from this brand's own material.
3. **Refine.** Lock the building blocks of the chosen direction.
4. **Reveal.** Mockups of the brand on the user's priority touchpoints.
5. **Brief.** The Brand Style Brief and its design tokens.

---

## Think like a designer, not a collector

A collector gathers looks they like and stitches them together. A designer finds the truth of the brand and builds the look from it. Work like a designer:

1. **Insight first.** Collect the truths about this brand: who it serves, where it shows up, how its product is made, where it comes from, what it believes, what its name contains. Every visual idea should grow out of one of these. Michael Bierut's version: the problem contains the solution, so dig into the problem before you sketch.
2. **Concept before style.** Before choosing colors or fonts, put the idea in one sentence, such as "bread built properly". Style is how that idea gets executed.
3. **A reason for every choice.** Each recommendation carries a "because" tied to this brand: an audience need, a touchpoint constraint, an origin detail, a positioning goal. "Because it's trendy" and "because Brand X does it" are not reasons.
4. **The swap test.** Mentally put a competitor's name on the design. If it still fits, the design isn't tailored yet, so go back to the brand's specifics.
5. **Construct, don't borrow.** Build each element from the brand's own material:
   - draw the mark from the letters of the name, the product's shape, or a tool or mark from the making process
   - sample the palette from the place, the materials or the product
   - build patterns from the product's process or data
   - choose typefaces for the qualities the voice and the touchpoints need
6. **References reveal taste; they are never templates.** When the user mentions something they love, a brand included, find the quality that draws them (restraint, warmth, wit, craft, the way light falls). Then find this brand's own way to express that quality. Never recommend "do what X does", and never let a direction resemble a specific existing brand. A lookalike borrows someone else's recognition and makes the user look like the copy.
7. **Research to differ, not to imitate.** Studying competitors shows you what is already taken, so you can stay out of it.

Two examples of derivation:
- *A neighbourhood bakery whose founder trained as a structural engineer.* Idea: "bread built properly". That gives a strict grid, cross-section diagrams of loaves as the illustration style, and a palette of flour white, crust brown and blueprint blue. No wheat-sheaf badge.
- *An MCP server that reconciles invoices for other agents.* Idea: "everything balances". That gives a mark of two brackets meeting, a palette taken from ledger paper, tool names that read like ledger entries (`match_invoice`, `flag_mismatch`), and error messages that state exactly what didn't balance.

## Three designers' lenses, weighted by context

Judge every choice through the eyes of three designers whose methods pull in different directions. Each one asks a different question:

- **Massimo Vignelli: discipline.** He designed the 1972 New York subway map and the American Airlines identity, and wrote *The Vignelli Canon*. His question: *"Is this disciplined enough to still be right in twenty years?"*
  - a handful of typefaces used well beats many used badly
  - a grid under everything: structure before decoration
  - timeless over fashionable
  - one design language across every touchpoint ("if you can design one thing, you can design everything")
  - *Overused, it turns cold and anonymous.*
- **Paul Rand: the idea in the mark.** His logos include IBM, UPS, ABC, Westinghouse and NeXT. His question: *"Is there one simple idea people will remember, with room for it to earn its meaning?"*
  - a logo identifies; it doesn't have to explain the business, and it takes its meaning over time from the quality of what it stands for
  - simplicity with a touch of wit: he treated play as a serious tool
  - argue for the recommendation: he presented NeXT with a single logo and a booklet explaining his reasoning
  - *Overused, it shrinks the brand to a logo and forgets the system around it.*
- **Paula Scher: serious play.** Her work includes the Public Theater identity and the Citi logo, from decades at Pentagram. Her question: *"Is this serious without being solemn?"* Her TED talk contrasts solemn work, which is correct and acceptable, with serious work, which is intuitive, spontaneous and sometimes imperfect. Serious work is where the breakthroughs come from.
  - type can be the image: scale, weight and energy carry the voice
  - let intuition lead, then check it against the strategy
  - identity at full volume on walls, posters and environments
  - *Overused, it shouts over everyday screens and fights legibility.*

These lenses distill each designer's published work, writing and talks. Naming them is this skill's way of borrowing their methods; they didn't write or endorse it. Borrow how they decide, never what they made: no direction should look like a Vignelli map, a Rand logo or a Scher poster.

The lenses are not universal rules. Weight them by the brand's context before you recommend anything. A kids' brand judged by Vignelli alone comes out lifeless. An enterprise security brand led by Scher feels risky to the person signing.

| Brand context | Lead | Support | Selective |
|---|---|---|---|
| Enterprise B2B, regulated, B2G | Vignelli | Rand | Scher: launches, events, recruiting |
| Developer tools, APIs, M2M / MCP | Vignelli | Rand | Scher: docs, error pages, easter eggs |
| SMB and prosumer software | Rand | Vignelli | Scher: onboarding, empty states |
| D2C, packaged goods, hospitality | Scher | Rand | Vignelli: checkout, legal, support |
| Luxury | Vignelli | Rand | Scher: unboxing, events |
| Culture, arts, events | Scher | Vignelli | Rand: the core mark |
| Kids, games, education | Scher | Rand | Vignelli: parent and teacher pages |
| Nonprofit or cause | Rand | Vignelli | Scher: campaigns |
| Creative studio, personal brand | Scher | Rand | Vignelli: contracts, invoices |

Once the client model and personality are clear, state the weighting in a recap ("Led by Vignelli, supported by Rand, Scher for launches") and let the user adjust it. "More Scher" is a perfectly good instruction. Use the weighting everywhere: how bold each direction gets, how loud each mockup is, and what you critique before presenting.

### Spend expression where it counts

This is adapted from Emil Kowalski's frequency rule for animation: how often someone meets a touchpoint decides how loud it can be. Plan an **expression budget**:

| How often it's seen | Treatment | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Many times a day | Quiet: the brand gets out of the way | product UI, dashboards, tool results, receipts |
| Weekly | Recognizable, light touches | emails, social feed, docs |
| Occasionally | Confident: the full system | website, slides, signage |
| Rare or once | Expressive: the moment to surprise | unboxing, a card handed over, a launch, the 404 page, onboarding, merch |

A brand that shouts in its dashboard is tiring by Tuesday. A brand that whispers on its packaging disappears on the shelf. Use this table when you decide where the signature device and the boldest color go.

### The bench: whose method to borrow for a specific decision

| Decision | Whose method | What to borrow |

## How the interview works

### Map a design tree, work it in rounds

Every brand decision hangs off other decisions:
- the logo depends on where it will appear, and on the name
- the palette depends on the personality and on what competitors have already taken
- the typefaces depend on the touchpoints, the languages, the budget and who will be typing with them

Treat all of this as a **design tree**.

Work the tree in **rounds**. The *frontier* is every decision whose prerequisites are already settled: the questions you can ask now without guessing at answers you haven't heard. Ask the frontier as one round, **capped at 3–5 questions** so it stays easy to answer. If the frontier is bigger, ask the most load-bearing questions first. A question that depends on another one still open waits for a later round.

Format every question like this, with a blank line between the parts:

```
❓ **Q4 — <short title>**

<the question, in one or two sentences>

- **a)** <option, a few words>
- **b)** <option>
- **c)** <option>

*Why it matters:* <one line on what this changes in the design>

➡️ **My take:** <your specific recommendation and its reason, based on what you know so far>

---

❓ **Q5 — <short title>**

...
```

**Number questions across the whole session.** If round one ends at Q3, round two starts at Q4, and so on. Never restart at Q1. That way the user can point back to any earlier question ("go back to Q5"), an answer like "6: b" can't be confused with one from an earlier round, and the decision log can show where each decision was made.

Then stop and wait. Tell the user that shorthand answers are fine, using this round's numbers ("4: a, c — 5: your take — 6: not sure").

### Make every message easy to scan

The user reads a lot of questions, so readability is part of the job:
- **One option per line.** Show options as a bulleted list with a bold letter (`- **a)** Website`). Never run options together in a sentence or with separators like `·` or `/`. Lines that start with a bare "a)" are not enough either, because most chat apps merge consecutive lines into one paragraph.
- **Blank lines between parts.** The question, the options, *Why it matters* and *My take* each get their own paragraph, for the same reason.
- **Short options.** A few words each. Explanations go in the question or in your take, not in every option.
- **Group long lists.** When there are more than about 8 options, split them under short bold labels (such as **Digital**, **Print and physical**). Letters keep counting across the groups, and no group runs past about 8.
- **Flat structure.** One level of bullets in anything the user sees, with no lists inside lists.
- **Labelled fields as bullets.** Recaps and directions put one field per bullet.
- **Tables for comparisons**, such as competitor conventions or the directions side by side.

**Why recommend every time:** reacting is much easier than starting from a blank page. A recommendation shows your reasoning and lets the user answer "yes, that" and move fast. Make it specific and give the reason. "Cream #F4EFE6 with oxblood #6B1E1E, taken from the wine-stained oak of your tasting room" is useful. "A warm palette could work" is not.

If a multiple-choice tool is available (such as AskUserQuestion), you may use it for crisp option questions. Keep the running question number in each question's text. Keep open-ended questions in text.

### Facts are your job, decisions are theirs

Don't ask the user for anything you can find out yourself. If you have web search or fetch:
- **Their own presence.** Look at their website, social profiles or shared files before asking about them, and give an honest read of what is already there.
- **Competitors.** Scan the 5–8 most visible competitors and note their colors, type, logo styles, imagery and tone. This tells you what is already taken, so the brand can avoid it.
- **The name.** Check whether it is visibly in use in the same space. Flag what you find and make clear this is not a legal trademark clearance.
- **Culture.** Check what colors and symbols mean in the markets they named.

Do lookups between rounds and keep the user moving; only the questions that depend on a lookup should wait for it. If you can't search, ask the user to name competitors and describe them, and say what you would normally check.

Decisions about taste, priorities and trade-offs always go to the user.

### Grill: push back, kindly

The value of the interview comes from not accepting the first answer. Push when you see:

- **Vague adjectives.** Every brand wants to look "modern", "clean", "minimal", "premium", "bold", "fun" or "professional". Make the word concrete by offering two contrasting readings to choose from: "Clean like a Japanese tea room (natural, quiet, lots of air) or clean like a laboratory instrument (precise, engineered, gridded)?"
- **Category defaults.** When they pick what everyone in their category does, say so: "Five of your six competitors are navy with a geometric sans. Navy makes you invisible next to them. Is blending in the goal, or should we find a color that comes from your own story?" Blending in can be legitimate, for example to signal trust in a regulated category. It just has to be a conscious choice.
- **Contradictions.** "Luxury" with "affordable for everyone". "Playful" with an enterprise procurement buyer. "Minimal" with a homepage that must show twelve product lines. Surface the tension and ask which side wins, or how to hold both. A tension held on purpose ("precise but warm") is often the most distinctive thing about a brand.
- **Everything is a priority.** Make them rank: "If people could remember only one thing about this brand, what should it be?"
- **"Make it like [famous brand]."** Find which quality they mean (restraint? the photography? irreverence?). Then show how *this* brand can express that quality from its own material, and explain why a lookalike would hurt them.
- **"I don't know."** That's fine. Give your recommendation, log it as an assumption and move on. The directions and mockups will test it visually.

Keep pushback short and warm: one pointed challenge per issue, then respect the user's call. You are a sparring partner, not a gatekeeper.

### Keep a decision log

Track what is locked, what is still open and what you assumed. After every 2–3 rounds, and whenever the stage changes, show a short recap and ask the user to correct anything wrong. This keeps the session from drifting.

```
📌 **Locked so far**

- Priority touchpoints (Q1): packaging (shelf), Instagram, website
- Client (Q3): D2C + retail, 25–40 urban home cooks, mid-premium
- Brand truths (Q5): the founder's engineering past; dough scored with a ruler
- Personality (Q8): warm, a bit irreverent, crafted (not rustic)

❔ **Still open**

- Print-finish budget
- Whether the founder's face is part of the brand

⚠️ **Assumed**

- English-only at launch
```

### Show, don't just tell

People react to images faster and more honestly than to words. During the directions and refine stages, if you can render something (an inline SVG or HTML visual, an artifact, a generated image, a Figma frame), sketch quickly. Useful sketches:
- palette swatches with hex codes
- the brand name set in each candidate typeface
- a rough style tile per direction

Keep these rough. The full mockups come later, in the reveal. If you can't render, describe concretely: hex codes, typeface names, and one vivid sentence on how the shelf front or homepage hero would look.

### Let the user steer the pace

Be thorough by default, but say at the start: "Say *speed up* anytime and I'll make the calls and flag my assumptions. Say *go deeper* on anything."
- **Speed up.** Ask only the load-bearing questions (touchpoints; what it is and who it's for; brand truths; personality; hard constraints), then go to directions. Log the rest as assumptions in the brief.
- **Go deeper.** Use more of the question map and the idea generators on the branch the user cares about.
- **Narrow request** (only a palette, only a font pairing, only a logo direction). Grill just the branches that decision depends on, usually touchpoints, audience, category and personality, in 1–2 rounds before you recommend.

## If a brand already exists: audit before you ask

When the user is rebranding or has existing assets (a website, logo files, a deck, photos of packaging or signage), look before you ask. Review what's there and open with a short audit instead of a blank-slate round, so the questions build on evidence.

Check:
- **Equity:** what people already recognize and would miss
- **Consistency:** whether the touchpoints look like one brand, and which colors, fonts or logo versions have drifted
- **Distinctiveness:** which category clichés it relies on, and whether it passes the swap test
- **Legibility:** the mark at its smallest size, text contrast, readability on the hardest touchpoint
- **Gaps:** forgotten touchpoints running on defaults
- **What works:** strengths to keep, named explicitly

Present the findings by severity:

```
🔴 **Critical**

- <breaks trust or legibility, e.g. "the logo is unreadable at favicon size">

🟡 **Important**

- <hurts recognition or consistency>

🟢 **Opportunities**

- <could become an ownable asset>

✅ **Working well**

- <keep this>
```

Then give your read in a few lines: what the brand is today, the equity to keep, and the scale of change you'd recommend (a refresh, an evolution or a revolution). Ask the user to confirm or adjust before the first round. Skip any question the audit has already answered. If you can render, show the most serious finding next to your proposed fix.

## The opening round

Start by asking **where the brand will live**. Touchpoints ground everything in physical reality: a brand that lives on billboards and embroidered uniforms needs a very different mark from one that lives in an app and an MCP directory. They also decide which mockups you build at the end.

In the same round, ask about the user's **gut style**, before any analysis, so you have their raw instinct to pressure-test later. Ask about the **client model** too.

Before asking, read what the user has already said, and don't re-ask anything it answers. Acknowledge what you know and move the frontier on. If you know nothing about the brand yet, add a **Q0**: its name (if it has one), what it does in one sentence, and whether it's new or a rebrand.

## The question map

These branches are a **map, not a script**. Follow the dependencies, skip what's already answered, and go deepest where this brand's biggest risk is. Mix your own observations and provocations into each round, not just questions.

### 1. Touchpoints: where the brand lives

Ask:
- Which touchpoints carry the brand in year one, and which in three years? Which 2–3 must it win?
- What is the **smallest** application (favicon, avatar, directory icon, embroidery on a cuff, thermal receipt) and the **largest** (billboard, storefront, trade-show wall)?
- Who will produce these day to day: a designer, the founder in Canva, franchisees, a print shop, developers?

**Rule: design for the hardest touchpoint first.** If embroidery or a 16px icon is in the mix, the core mark must survive it. If outdoor is the priority, the brand needs one huge, simple, ownable element.

What each touchpoint demands:

**Digital**
- **Website / web app:** responsive logo lockups, web-font licensing, WCAG contrast (body text ≥ 4.5:1), dark mode, few font files so pages load fast.
- **Mobile app:** the icon must read without the wordmark, from 1024px down to about 29px, next to every other app on the home screen.
- **Social:** circular avatar crop; 1:1, 4:5 and 9:16 formats; video first; safe zones. In a fast scroll a distinctive color or device beats the logo.
- **Email:** limited font support (plan fallbacks), dark mode that inverts logos, images that may be switched off.
- **Digital ads:** tiny sizes and a split-second glance, so people must recognize the brand without reading.
- **Developer surfaces (docs, API reference, CLI, status pages):** a monospace pairing, ANSI terminal colors, a code-highlighting theme, a diagram style.
- **AI agents, MCP servers and connector directories:**
  - the icon and listing in directories and registries
  - the server's name, tool names and descriptions
  - how the brand is described inside AI answers

  See M2M below.
- **Video, podcast, YouTube:** animated logo, lower thirds, thumbnails, cover art that works at 3000px and at thumbnail size, a sonic logo.

**Print and physical**
- **Business cards & stationery:** CMYK or Pantone, paper stock, finishes (letterpress, foil, emboss, edge painting, duplex board), minimum type sizes. A card is a tactile brand moment.
- **Packaging & labels:**
  - shelf impact from about 3 m, and the blink test (recognized in under a second)
  - dielines, and a variant system for flavors and sizes
  - space for regulatory text
  - substrate (kraft, clear, metal, glass), which shifts colors
  - the unboxing
- **Outdoor (billboards, posters, transit):** read in about 3–5 seconds at a distance, about 7 words maximum, huge contrast, simple shapes.
- **Storefront, signage, interiors:** fabrication (channel letters, neon or LED, laser-cut, hand-painted), night illumination, local sign rules, wayfinding, materials.
- **Vehicles:** seen in motion, from the side, on curved surfaces broken by doors and handles.
- **Merch, uniforms, apparel:** embroidery loses fine detail and gradients; screen printing allows only a few spot colors.
- **Events & booths:** large format, seen from across an aisle under harsh lighting.
- **Point of sale, menus, receipts, tickets, stickers:** thermal receipts print one color at low resolution.
- **The product itself:** embossing on hardware, LED colors, on-device screens, labels and tags.
- **Presentations & documents:** templates other people will fill in. Fonts must exist in PowerPoint or Google Slides, or be embedded.

**Sensory:** sound (sonic logo, UI sounds, hold music), scent (retail and hospitality), touch (materials and finishes).

**Forgotten touchpoints.** Brands most often disappear in moments nobody designed. Check which of these exist:
- the favicon, and the preview image that shows when a link is shared
- email signatures, calendar invites, booking pages
- invoices, quotes, receipts, packing slips, shipping labels and tape
- confirmation, password-reset and "your order has shipped" emails
- 404, empty, loading and error states
- video-call backgrounds, and the slide template other people will use
- for MCP servers: error messages, empty results, first-run setup

Each one either carries the brand or falls back to a default. Pick the gaps worth closing and add them to the touchpoint list. Wally Olins counted behavior as part of identity, next to products, environments and communications, so how a complaint gets answered belongs here too.

### 2. Gut style and references

- "Describe the feel in 3–5 words." Then grill the words.
- "What do you love the look of, from places, objects, materials, buildings, films and eras to brands, and what *exactly* do you love about each?" The answer you need is the quality ("how little is on the page", "the way the light falls", "how confident the type is"), not the thing. Write down the quality and leave the source behind.
- "Name 3 looks you never want." Anti-references often reveal more than likes.
- "Is anything already decided or emotionally attached?" A color the founder loves, an existing logo, the typeface from the pitch deck. Attachments are real, so find them early.
- Optional quick-fire *this-or-that* pairs: serif or sans? Photography or illustration? Dark or light? Dense or airy? Color-rich or restrained? Handmade or engineered?

### 3. Client model: who's on the other side

Present the model's implications, then ask follow-ups.

- **B2B.** The buyer is a committee (user, champion, economic buyer, IT and security, procurement) and the cycle is long.
  - The brand must signal competence, stability and clarity.
  - It lives in sales decks, LinkedIn, webinars, trade shows, product UI and docs.
  - Enterprise leans toward restraint; SMB toward approachable and fast.
  - *Opportunity:* most B2B brands look alike, so a human, expressive one stands out, provided it still feels safe to the person signing.
- **B2C.** Fast, emotional decisions. The brand must stop the scroll or win the shelf, and distinctive assets do more work than explanation. Social, packaging and community matter most.
- **D2C.** The brand *is* the store. Unboxing, social content and the founder's story carry it, and it must stay consistent across a firehose of content.
- **B2B2C / white-label / franchise.** Two audiences. Decide the brand architecture: co-branded, endorsed or invisible ("powered by"). The system must work next to partners' logos.
- **B2M (business-to-machine).** A business sells to AI agents or automated buyers acting for people.
  - The brand must be machine-legible: the same name and description everywhere, structured data, crisp specs, predictable product and plan names.
  - People still set up and approve the agents, so trust signals for them still matter.
- **M2M (machine-to-machine), for example MCP to MCP.** Software sits on both sides: an AI agent or MCP client discovers your MCP server, decides which tool to call and reads what comes back. Other cases include agent-to-agent protocols (such as A2A agent cards), APIs and IoT. Here the brand is read by models first and humans second, and most of it is structural and verbal:
  - **Identity inside the protocol.**
    - The server's name and display title.
    - A consistent tool-naming scheme (e.g. `verb_noun`).
    - Tool descriptions: the "packaging" a model reads when deciding what to call.
    - The same parameter vocabulary across tools, predictable result shapes, error messages that say what went wrong and how to fix it, server instructions, versioning.

    Consistency *is* the brand: an agent keeps choosing a server whose tools behave predictably.
  - **Write for models, not as marketing.** Descriptions must be precise and specific. Brand voice shows in clarity, naming and consistency, not in adjectives.
  - **Small visual surfaces.** The icon in connector directories, registries and client UIs must read at 16–32px on both light and dark backgrounds. There is also the listing card and the sign-in or consent screen.
  - **Human surfaces.** For the developers and admins who find, install and approve it: README, docs, registry listing, changelog, status page.
  - **Status colors.** Red, amber and green already mean something in dashboards and logs, so a red brand color will fight error states.
- **B2G / public sector.** Accessibility is mandatory. Sobriety, trust and procurement documents dominate.
- **Nonprofit / cause.** Two audiences, donors and beneficiaries. Show beneficiaries with dignity, avoid pity-driven imagery, and make transparency visible.
- **Marketplace / platform.** The brand often steps back so that users' content can shine: a frame, not the painting.
- **Personal brand (creator, consultant, freelancer).** The person's face and voice are the brand, so the identity frames them rather than competing with them.
- **Employer / internal brand.** Recruiting, culture, internal tools.

Many brands are hybrids, so ask which audience leads in year one. If the user's meaning of an acronym isn't clear from context, confirm it in one line.

Follow-ups:
- Describe the primary person (or agent): role, life stage, what they care about, where they first meet the brand, what they use today.
- What must they **feel in the first five seconds**? Safe, excited, understood, impressed, amused, calm?
- Price position: budget, mid, premium or luxury? Should the look match the price, or deliberately punch above it?
- Geography, languages and scripts. Latin only, or also Arabic and Hebrew (right-to-left), Chinese, Japanese or Korean, Devanagari, Cyrillic? Scripts narrow typeface choices fast.
- Cultural color meanings: check rather than assume. White is tied to mourning in parts of East Asia, red to luck and celebration in China, and green has religious significance in some Muslim-majority countries.

### 4. The business and its story

This branch is where the brand's raw material comes from. Dig hardest here.

- What does it do, in one sentence a 12-year-old would get?
- Why does it exist: the problem, the founder's itch, the belief?
- **Origin material:**
  - where it was founded, and that place's visual vernacular (signage, tiles, transit maps, landscape colors)
  - the founder's craft or previous career
  - how the product is made: its tools, marks, measurements, materials and ingredients
  - rituals, inside jokes, odd habits
- **The name:**
  - meaning, etymology, translations, length, pronunciation
  - its letters: symmetry, interesting shapes, double letters, a strong O, X or Q, ascenders
  - is it locked?
- **New or rebrand?** If a rebrand:
  - what do customers already recognize (a color, symbol, mascot or jingle)?
  - what must survive?
  - why change now?
  - evolution or revolution?
- **Ambition in 3–5 years:** more products, sub-brands, new countries, an exit? The system has to stretch that far.
- **Brand architecture:** one master brand, sub-brands, or named products?
- **Values:** keep only the ones they can prove with a behavior. Skip platitudes like "innovation, integrity, passion".

### 5. Market and category

- Who are the 3–8 competitors or alternatives, including indirect ones ("a spreadsheet", "doing nothing", "the corner shop")?
- Research them yourself where possible and build a **conventions table** of what's already taken:

  | Competitor | Colors | Type | Logo style | Imagery | Tone |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|

- Ask which conventions to **keep** and which to **break**. A kept cue tells people what kind of business this is; a broken cue gets noticed. A good brand usually keeps 1–2 cues and breaks the rest on purpose, replacing them with something from its own story.
- Positioning: on which dimension is this brand the obvious choice? Fastest, warmest, most expert, cheapest, most beautiful, most ethical, funniest?
- Finish Marty Neumeier's onlyness statement: "[Brand] is the only [category] that [benefit]." If it can't be finished without hedging, the positioning isn't sharp yet.
- What is the one thing competitors can't say?

### 6. Personality

Pick the 2–3 lenses that suit this user rather than running them all.

- **Sliders (fastest).** Pick the 5–6 sliders that matter most for this brand from the pool below, never all fourteen at once. Show one slider per line and ask for a 1–5 score on each, so a quick reply like "2, 4, 5, 1, 3" works:

  ```
  - **Serious ↔ Playful:** 1 – 2 – 3 – 4 – 5
  - **Quiet ↔ Loud:** 1 – 2 – 3 – 4 – 5
  ```

  The pool: Classic ↔ Contemporary, Serious ↔ Playful, Exclusive ↔ Inclusive, Quiet ↔ Loud, Refined ↔ Raw, Geometric ↔ Organic, Rational ↔ Emotional, Minimal ↔ Maximal, Corporate ↔ Indie, Global ↔ Local, High-tech ↔ Handmade, Calm ↔ Energetic, Soft ↔ Sharp, Mature ↔ Youthful.

  Push back on a page of 3s: a slider full of 3s is a beige brand. Ask them to commit to at least three extremes.
- **This, not that.** 4–6 pairs: "Confident, not arrogant. Warm, not cute. Expert, not academic."
- **Archetypes, as a lens rather than a box.** Sage, Explorer, Creator, Ruler, Caregiver, Innocent, Everyperson, Jester, Lover, Magician, Outlaw, Hero. Pick a primary and a secondary.
- **"If the brand were a…"** person at a party, car, hotel, restaurant, musician, city, decade, material, piece of furniture, animal. Ask three and push for specifics: "a 1970s Milanese espresso bar" beats "Italian". Treat the answers as clues to the brand's character, not as looks to reproduce.
- **The tension.** Which two qualities that rarely go together does this brand hold? Clinical and joyful, luxurious and irreverent, technical and tender.

### 7. Visual building blocks

Lock these while refining the chosen direction. Each gets options, a recommendation and its reason.

**Logo / identifier**
- **Types:**
  - wordmark: best for a distinctive, short name
  - lettermark or monogram: for long names, or when a small icon is needed
  - pictorial symbol
  - abstract mark
  - mascot or character: memorable and warm, but harder to keep consistent
  - emblem or badge: heritage and craft, but poor at small sizes
  - combination mark
  - dynamic or generative identity: changes with context around a fixed core
- **Build the idea from the brand's material:** the letters of the name, the product's shape, a tool or mark from the making process, a detail of the place, the core idea in one gesture.
- **Ask:** does the name need to be *read* (it's new) or only *recognized* (it's known)? Is a standalone icon needed? Which touchpoint is hardest?
- **Plan the system:** primary lockup, horizontal and stacked versions, symbol alone, favicon or app icon, one-color and reversed versions, clear space, minimum size.
- **Test it** against Sagi Haviv's criteria: appropriate, distinctive and memorable, simple. A logo can say very little, so decide which one idea it carries. Could someone doodle it from memory?

**Color**
- **Derive it.** Sample from the brand's world (its materials, place, product, process), then tune for contrast and production.
- **Strategies:**
  - one hero color owned outright and used with total consistency
  - monochrome plus one accent
  - an unexpected duo
  - full spectrum
  - muted or tonal
  - dark-mode first
  - a color-coded system for product lines
- **Ask:** the emotional target, which colors competitors already own, production limits (spot colors, embroidery thread, one-color receipts), dark mode, cultural meanings.
- **When recommending:**
  - Give hex values with roles: primary, secondary, accent, neutrals, and functional colors (success, warning, error, info).
  - Check contrast: body text ≥ 4.5:1, large text ≥ 3:1. Keep status colors distinguishable for color-blind users.
  - Treat neutrals as half the palette. Warm versus cool grays, and off-white versus pure white, carry a lot of personality.
  - Build tints in a perceptual color space (OKLCH) so they step evenly.
  - Flag that the same hex looks different on screen, on coated and uncoated paper, and on kraft, so print needs physical proofs and Pantone matching.

**Typography**
- **Classifications and how they read:**
  - geometric sans: modern, friendly, techy, and very common
  - grotesque / neo-grotesque: neutral, Swiss, confident
  - humanist sans: warm, readable
  - old-style and transitional serifs: literary, trustworthy, editorial
  - didone: fashion and luxury, but fragile at small sizes
  - slab: sturdy, industrial
  - monospace: technical, honest
  - display or experimental: personality, used sparingly
  - script or handwritten: personal, hard to scale
  - variable fonts: motion, responsive type
- **Choose for qualities:** the voice, the touchpoints, the scripts needed. Then check the choice isn't what competitors use.
- **Ask:** what is the licensing budget (free, commercial or custom)? Who will type with it? Non-designers in Google Docs or PowerPoint need fonts available there, or fallbacks.
- **Distinctiveness.** The most common faces (Inter, Montserrat, Poppins, Roboto, Open Sans, Lato, Playfair Display) are fine workhorses but won't make a brand distinctive. If you use one, let something else carry the personality.
- **Less-overused starting points.** Always verify the current license and script support.
  - *Free on Google Fonts:* Fraunces, Instrument Serif, Young Serif, Newsreader, Gloock, DM Serif Display, Bricolage Grotesque, Familjen Grotesk, Schibsted Grotesk, Space Grotesk, Syne, Unbounded, IBM Plex (Sans, Serif, Mono), JetBrains Mono
  - *Free on Fontshare:* Satoshi, General Sans, Switzer, Cabinet Grotesk, Clash Display, Zodiak, Erode, Gambetta, Boska
  - *Commercial:*
    - Klim: Söhne, Tiempos, Signifier
    - Grilli Type: GT America, GT Sectra, GT Alpina
    - Dinamo: ABC Diatype, ABC Favorit
    - Commercial Type: Canela, Druk
    - CoType: Aeonik
    - Pangram Pangram: PP Neue Montreal, PP Editorial New
- **Typographic signatures** can be the brand on their own: tight all-caps, a custom ligature, oversized numerals, a serif italic set against a grotesk, one altered letter in the wordmark that echoes the product.

**Shape language and graphic devices**
- **Corner radius** is a personality setting: 0 reads precise and architectural, 4–8px friendly-professional, full pill soft and playful. Pick one and apply it everywhere: buttons, photo crops, cards, die-cuts.
- **Base shapes:**
  - circles: community, completeness
  - squares: stability, order
  - triangles and arrows: direction, energy
  - organic blobs: approachable, but overused in tech
  - lines: precision
- **Graphic devices, each built from the brand:**
  - a frame derived from the mark
  - a crop rule (images always cropped into the symbol's shape)
  - a supergraphic: a giant fragment of the mark bleeding off the edges
  - a pattern built from a letter of the name or a mark from the process
  - a texture from the product's material
  - a highlight or underline treatment
  - labels as a system
  - color-block layouts

**Imagery**
- **Photography.** Decide:
  - the subject: people, product, place, process, details
  - the light: natural, flash, hard studio shadows
  - the grade: warm film, cool clinical, saturated, washed out
  - the composition
  - the casting: real customers or models

  Ban the clichés: handshakes, people pointing at laptops, glowing AI brains, smiling headset-wearing support agents.
- **Illustration styles:** flat vector, line, editorial hand-drawn, collage, risograph and grain, 3D or clay, isometric, pixel, technical or blueprint, scientific or botanical. Avoid flat faceless people with tiny heads and long limbs, often called "Corporate Memphis", which has become a cliché.
- **AI-generated imagery:** set a policy (allowed? disclosed?) and lock one consistent style if it's used.
- **Charts and data visualization** are a brand surface for B2B and M2M, so give them a style too.

**Iconography:** line or filled, stroke weight matched to the type, corners matched to the shape language. Draw custom icons, or adapt a library such as Phosphor, Lucide or Material Symbols.

**Layout, grid and spacing**
- Dense or airy? Centered and calm, or asymmetric and editorial? Type-led or image-led?
- Use a **spacing scale** built from one base unit (4 or 8 px), so the rhythm is consistent on every page.
- **Color proportion:** roughly 60% dominant, 30% secondary, 10% accent is a starting point.
- Whitespace signals confidence and luxury.

**Motion** is part of the identity, not decoration added at the end.
- **Derive it from the personality.** Snappy and springy reads energetic or playful. A slow ease-out reads calm or premium. Linear reads mechanical. Bounce belongs to playful brands and celebration moments, never to everyday actions in a serious brand.
- **Apply Emil Kowalski's frequency rule.** Actions people repeat all day (menus, toggles, keyboard shortcuts) get little or no motion. Rare moments (the logo reveal, a launch, onboarding, a success state) can be expressive. The best motion in everyday UI goes unnoticed.
- **Set motion tokens:** 2–3 durations (for example 150 ms small, 250 ms medium, 400 ms large) and 1–2 custom easing curves instead of the browser default.
- **Keep exits quieter than entrances.** Start scale animations from about 0.9–0.95, never from zero.
- **Pick one signature behavior,** such as how the logo resolves or how panels arrive, and reuse it.
- **Respect reduced-motion settings** in everything you build.
- If the **design-motion-principles** skill is available, use it for the motion layer of interactive mockups.

**Sound** (video, apps, retail, podcasts): a 2–3 second sonic logo, UI sounds, a music mood.

**Materials and finishes** (physical brands):
- Paper: uncoated reads natural and crafted; coated reads sharp and commercial.
- Finishes: foil, emboss and deboss, spot UV, letterpress, edge painting.
- Substrates: kraft, recycled, metal, wood, fabric.
- A sustainability stance, kept honest.

### 8. Verbal identity (usually worth one round)

Visual and verbal identity have to agree. A playful look with stiff copy feels fake.
- Tone sliders: formal ↔ casual, serious ↔ funny, respectful ↔ irreverent, enthusiastic ↔ matter-of-fact.
- Words to own; words to ban.
- Tagline, if any; naming conventions for products and plans.
- Test: rewrite one boring line ("Your order has shipped", "Error 404", "Thanks for subscribing") in the brand's voice.
- **For M2M / MCP, the verbal identity is most of the brand.** Settle:
  - the tool-naming scheme
  - a house style for tool descriptions (what each must say, in what order)
  - a consistent parameter vocabulary
  - the voice of error messages

### 9. Practicalities and constraints

- **Budget and timeline.** These decide custom type, print finishes, photo shoots and illustration commissions.
- **Who maintains the brand?** A founder in Canva needs a simple, template-friendly system. An in-house team can run a richer one.
- **Tools in use** (Canva, Figma, Google Workspace, PowerPoint, Shopify, Webflow…). Fonts and colors must work in them.
- **Existing assets** that must be kept or used up.
- **Accessibility beyond the baseline:** older audiences, low vision, dyslexia-friendly needs.
- **Legal.** Trademark searches for the name and mark belong to a professional: flag the need, don't give legal advice. Check font and image licenses.
- **Rules from a parent brand, franchise or partner.**
- **Mockup content.** The name, title and contact details for the business card; the main offer and headline for the landing page; a real topic for the slides. Ask once near the end of the interview. If the user would rather not say, use clearly marked placeholders.
- **Success criteria.** How will they know in six months that the brand works? It's recognized without the logo? The deck lands? People pick it off the shelf? Agents keep choosing the server?

## Making it unique: idea generators

Every generator starts from **this brand's own material**, the truths you collected in the interview, never from another brand's look. Uniqueness rarely comes from a wild logo. It comes from one or two **ownable assets**, built from the brand's truth and used with discipline. Drop the best ideas into rounds as provocations along the way, for example: "What if the whole system came from the ruler marks you score the dough with?"

1. **Audit the conventions, then zag.** List what everyone in the category does. Keep 1–2 cues so people know what you are. Replace the rest with things from the brand's own story.
2. **Mine the origin.** The place, the process, the founder, the name. Real specifics can't be copied, and they come with a story built in.
3. **Translate through an unrelated world.** Find a world that shares one of the brand's values but looks nothing like its category, and translate the value, not the look. A bank that is patient and careful might take its rigor from botanical field notes. A law firm that prizes clarity might borrow the logic of wayfinding systems.
4. **Hold a tension.** Pair two qualities that rarely meet and make every element carry both halves, for example a strict grid with one hand-drawn accent.
5. **Constrain hard.** One color only. One typeface in one weight. Photos shot only from directly above, or only at golden hour. Everything lowercase. Constraints create recognition.
6. **Find the distinctive asset.** Ask what people could recognize with the logo covered up: a color, a frame, a pattern, a character, a typographic quirk, a photo rule, a sound, a motion, a material, a phrase. Pick 1–2 and repeat them relentlessly.
7. **Use an era or movement as a tool, with a reason.** Choose it because its ideas match the brand's values, then adapt it to the brand's material. A pastiche is a costume. Options include:
   - Swiss International Style, Bauhaus, Constructivism
   - Art Deco, Art Nouveau, Arts & Crafts, mid-century modern
   - 70s supergraphics, Memphis, 90s acid graphics, Y2K, brutalism
   - Japanese *ma* minimalism, risograph zine, psychedelia, solarpunk, retro-futurism
   - folk and vernacular craft, scientific diagrams, editorial magazine, maximalism

   Say how the choice will age.
8. **Write a reverse brief.** Ask: "Describe the most generic, forgettable version of this brand." Then do the opposite on 2–3 axes.
9. **Let the product be the identity.** Can the system come from the product's own form: its UI, its silhouette, a pattern its data makes, the marks its process leaves, the shape of its API?
10. **Make it behave.** Design a dynamic identity that changes with the time, the weather, the data, the user or the season, around a fixed core.
11. **Pull the sincerity lever.** In a serious category, one moment of wit (a 404 page, a packaging flap, an error message) can make the whole brand memorable.

## Directions: diverge, then converge

When the strategy recap is confirmed (touchpoints, client and audience, brand truths, category, personality, constraints), propose **three genuinely different directions**, not three tints of one idea. **All three grow from this brand's own truths.** They differ in which truth they build on, and how boldly.

1. **Rooted.** Reads at once as its category, but every element is built from this brand's specifics rather than the category's stock imagery. Lowest risk.
2. **The Zag.** Breaks the category's most important conventions and replaces them with something from the brand's story. Most distinctive. The name is Marty Neumeier's word for radical differentiation.
3. **The Wildcard.** Grows from the brand's most unexpected material: an origin detail, the tension it holds, a quirk of its name. It might be wrong, but it often holds the best idea to keep.

Present each direction like this:

```
### Direction A — <evocative name, 2–4 words>

- **Built from:** the brand truths it grows out of
- **Lens weighting:** e.g. led by Scher, supported by Rand
- **Concept:** 1–2 sentences: the idea, and why it fits this brand
- **Feels like:** 3 adjectives
- **Logo approach:** type of mark + the idea behind it
- **Type:** headline face + body face (+ mono if needed), and why
- **Signature device:** the ownable asset
- **Imagery:** the photo or illustration rule in one line
- **Shape & motion:** corner radius, shape language, motion character
- **Voice sample:** one headline written in this direction
- **On your #1 touchpoint:** how it looks there
- **Swap test:** why this couldn't belong to a competitor
- **Risk:** what could go wrong, and who might not like it

| Color | Hex | Role | Comes from |
|---|---|---|---|
| <name> | <#hex> | <primary, accent…> | <the brand truth it's sampled from> |
```

After the three directions, add a short comparison table with one row per direction (concept, palette in a few words, signature device, risk) so the user can compare them at a glance. If you can render, show each direction as a rough style tile.

Then grill the choice: what do they love in each, and what do they hate? The answer is often "A's palette with C's device". That's fine, but check that the mix is still one coherent idea, because a hybrid can lose what made each direction work. Then run 1–3 rounds on the chosen direction to lock each building block from section 7.

## The reveal: show the brand in use

Once the refined direction is confirmed, stop describing and show. Seeing the brand on a real page or card is where people decide whether they believe in it:
- a **landing page** shows colors, type, spacing and buttons working together
- a **business card** lets them picture handing it over in a meeting
- a **slide** shows whether the brand holds up in front of a client

### Pick what to build from their touchpoints

Mock up the 2–3 touchpoints the user starred in the opening round, or more if they ask. Don't build things they never mentioned: a business card means little to a brand that only lives in an MCP directory. If their priorities are unclear, a landing page plus a business card is a good default. Add a short slide deck for B2B, or for anyone who pitches.

| Touchpoint | Mockup | What it lets the user judge |
|---|---|---|
| Website / web app | Landing page: nav with logo, hero with headline and buttons, features with cards and icons, an imagery block, a proof or testimonial section, a call-to-action band, footer | color proportions, type hierarchy, spacing rhythm, button states, the voice in context |
| Business cards & stationery | Front and back at true proportion (85 × 55 mm in Europe, 3.5 × 2 in in the US), shown lying on a surface, with a note on stock and finish | how it feels to hand over; the mark at small size |
| Presentations | 4–6 slides at 16:9: title, section divider, text + image, a chart, a quote, closing / contact | whether the system survives real content and data |
| Social | Avatar in a circular crop + 3 post templates (announcement, quote, product) | recognition in a feed |
| Mobile app | App icon at large and small sizes on a home screen + one key screen | the mark without the wordmark; the UI feel |
| Packaging & labels | Front panel or label on the real product shape, plus one variant | shelf impact, hierarchy, the variant system |
| Outdoor | One billboard or poster, at most 7 words | readability at a glance |
| Signage / storefront | Fascia or sign elevation, by day and by night | scale, fabrication, illumination |
| Email | Newsletter header and first section, light and dark mode | font fallbacks, logo inversion |
| Merch / uniforms | Tee, tote or cap with the embroidery-safe mark | simplification at stitched sizes |
| M2M / MCP | Directory listing card with the icon at 16, 32 and 64 px on light and dark backgrounds; a README or docs header; two sample tool definitions (name, title, description) and one error message in the brand's verbal system | legibility to models, and to the people who install it |

### Build every mockup from one system

Before building anything, write the direction down as **design tokens**, and build every mockup from them. That way the user sees one coherent system rather than unrelated pictures. In Michael Bierut's terms, an identity system is the launch pad, not the rocket ship: it has to keep working day after day in other people's hands. Tokens to define:
- **Color:** each color with its role (background, surface, text, primary, accent, success / warning / error)
- **Type:** families, weights, and a type scale (e.g. ratio 1.25: 16 · 20 · 25 · 31 · 39 · 49 px)
- **Spacing:** a base unit and its scale (e.g. 4 · 8 · 12 · 16 · 24 · 32 · 48 · 64 · 96 px)
- **Radius, borders and shadows**
- **Motion:** durations and easing

In HTML, put these as CSS custom properties at the top of the file. A small "brand sheet" section at the end of the landing page, with swatches, a type specimen and the spacing scale, helps the user see the tokens directly.

### Make it real

- **Real content in the brand's voice:** the actual name, offer, product names and headlines. Never lorem ipsum, which hides whether the voice works.
- **The logo is an original concept sketch**, built from the brand's own material (simple SVG from type and geometry). Label it as a concept a designer can refine.
- **Imagery.** Without real photos, use the graphic devices, or placeholder frames carrying an art-direction caption ("Photo: flour-dusted hands, hard morning side light, warm grade"), so the image rule is still visible.
- **Accessible by default:** text contrast ≥ 4.5:1, readable sizes, visible focus states.
- **Check every mockup by looking at it before you show it.** Render it or take a screenshot if you can, and confirm the fonts loaded, nothing overflows, the contrast holds and the card is at true proportion.

### Check against the generic AI look

Generated mockups drift toward the same handful of patterns, and that look is the opposite of a unique brand. None of them is wrong once, with a reason. Repetition and defaults are what make them generic. Find and fix:

- **Default palette:** purple-to-blue gradients, gradient text in headlines, neon glows on dark backgrounds, when the brand truths didn't produce them
- **Default type:** one common sans for everything, at similar weights, with no typographic signature
- **The template page:** centered hero, three feature cards with an icon in a rounded square, a logo wall, a testimonial carousel, then a call-to-action band. Use the sections the brand needs, in the order its story needs.
- **The same treatment everywhere:** identical radius, shadow and hover-scale on every element, the same fade-up on every section, a stagger on every list. Fix it when 3 or more components share an effect for no reason.
- **Decoration without meaning:** emoji or generic icons as feature illustrations, abstract blobs, frosted-glass cards, sparkle icons, "✨ New" pill badges
- **Copy on autopilot:** "Unlock the power of…", "Transform your workflow", "seamless", "revolutionary", "elevate". If a competitor could say the line, rewrite it.
- **Pulsing, glowing or looping effects** that exist only to look alive

Never invent testimonials, customer quotes or client logos and present them as real. Use clearly labelled placeholders ("Placeholder: a quote from a real customer").

### Craft details that make a mockup feel finished

- **Type:**
  - body text at least 16 px on screen
  - lines of about 45–75 characters (Robert Bringhurst's range)
  - line height about 1.5 for body text and 1.1–1.2 for large headlines
  - tighter tracking on big headlines, looser on small capitals
  - tabular figures in tables and prices
- **Optical alignment:** trust the eye over the grid. Round letters and shapes need a slight overshoot to look the same size as square ones. Play buttons and arrows need nudging to look centered, and buttons with icons need a little less padding on the icon side.
- **Nested corners:** an inner radius equals the outer radius minus the padding between them. Otherwise the corners look pinched.
- **Depth:** on photos and varied backgrounds, soft layered shadows sit better than hard borders. Keep to one elevation style.
- **Consistency:** every value comes from the tokens, with no one-off colors, sizes or spacings.
- **The mark at its extremes:** check the logo at favicon size and at its largest, in one color and reversed.

### Use the best tool available

Use what the environment offers:
- a design or slides tool, or an artifact type for designs or slides
- a connected design app such as Figma
- otherwise, self-contained HTML files (web fonts from Google Fonts or Fontshare are fine)

For the presentation, make a PowerPoint (.pptx) file when the user wants to edit it in PowerPoint. Otherwise use whatever slide tool is available. Fonts in a .pptx must be installed on the viewer's machine or embedded, so specify fallbacks.

### Present like a designer

Before presenting, critique your own work through the three lenses: what Vignelli would remove or tighten, what Rand would simplify into one clearer idea, and where Scher would turn the volume up. Fix what you find. When the user wants only your recommendation, present it the way Rand presented NeXT: one solution, with the full reasoning.

Walk the user through each mockup the way a designer presents to a client: 2–3 lines on what to notice and why it is there, traced back to the brand. For example: "The back of the card is solid oxblood with the mark debossed: the one moment of richness, because clients receive it after a tasting."

Then run a feedback round in the usual format: what to keep and what to change, each with your recommendation. Iterate on the mockups until the user is happy, then write the brief.

## Ending: the Brand Style Brief

The work is done when every branch that matters for this brand has been visited, nothing has been silently assumed, and the user is happy with the mockups. Write the brief in the best format the environment offers: a document or artifact if available, otherwise structured markdown or a file.

```
# <Brand> — Brand Style Brief

## 1. Snapshot
One paragraph: what the brand is, who it's for, and the idea that makes it different.

## 2. Strategy
- What we do / why we exist
- Client model and audience (primary, secondary); what they must feel in 5 seconds
- Positioning: the dimension we win on
- Category conventions we keep / break
- Personality: sliders, this-not-that, archetype, the tension we hold

## 3. Where the brand lives
Touchpoints in priority order, each with the requirement it puts on the system; the hardest touchpoint and how we solve it.

## 4. Creative concept
The idea in one sentence; the brand truths it's built from; the 1–2 distinctive assets to repeat relentlessly.

## 5. Visual identity
Each element with a one-line rationale tied to the brand.
- Logo: type, idea, lockups, minimum sizes, one-color and reversed versions, clear space
- Color: table (name · hex · RGB · CMYK/Pantone to verify · role · rough usage share · where it comes from); approved text/background pairs with contrast ratios; colors to avoid
- Typography: faces, weights, roles, hierarchy, fallbacks, licensing notes
- Shape language and graphic devices
- Imagery: photography rules, illustration rules, banned clichés
- Iconography
- Layout, grid and spacing
- Motion and sound
- Materials and finishes (if physical)

## 6. Design tokens
The color roles, type scale, spacing scale, radius, shadows and motion values the mockups use.

## 7. Voice
Tone, words to use and avoid, sample lines; for M2M, the naming scheme and description style.

## 8. Applications
The mockups delivered, with notes; guidance for touchpoints not yet mocked up.

## 9. Do / Don't
6–10 concrete rules.

## 10. Open questions and assumptions

## 11. Next steps
```

Then offer **one or two next steps** that fit the tools available:
- mock up more touchpoints
- export the tokens for developers
- build editable templates (social posts, slides, documents)
- write a brief for a designer to refine the logo
- draft fuller voice guidelines

Mention two cheap safeguards:
- a professional trademark search before investing in the name and mark
- showing the mockups to five real target customers and asking "what kind of company is this, and who is it for?" before rolling out

## Things to avoid

- **Copying or resembling.** No direction, logo or mockup should evoke a specific existing brand or a famous designer's specific piece, and "do what X does" is never a recommendation. Never reproduce logos, trademarks or mascots. Learning from how designers work is encouraged; reproducing what they made is not.
- **Choices without a reason from this brand.** If you can't say which brand truth a choice comes from, it isn't finished.
- **Dumping 15 questions at once, or dripping one tiny question per message forever.** Rounds of 3–5.
- **Walls of text:** options run together on one line, question numbers that restart each round, lists inside lists. See "Make every message easy to scan".
- **Generic recommendations** like "a modern, clean palette". Every recommendation should be specific enough to execute.
- **Your own favorite aesthetic, or the trend of the moment,** as the default. If you use a trend, say so and say how it will age.
- **Mockups of touchpoints the user doesn't have,** or mockups filled with lorem ipsum.
- **Forgetting accessibility:** contrast, legibility at small sizes, color-blind-safe status colors, reduced motion.
- **Losing the thread** in a long session. Re-read the decision log before recommending, building mockups or writing the brief.

## Before you call it done

- Every major choice traces to a named brand truth, and the design passes the swap test.
- The Vignelli / Rand / Scher weighting fits the brand's context, and expression is spent where people meet the brand rarely.
- The mockups cover the user's starred touchpoints, use real copy and are built from the tokens.
- Nothing from the generic-AI-look list survives without a reason.
- Contrast, minimum sizes and reduced motion are checked.
- The brief records the decisions, the tokens, the assumptions and the open questions.
